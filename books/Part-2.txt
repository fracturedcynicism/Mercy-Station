PART TWO: THE INVESTIGATION
CHAPTER NINE: THE RECONSTRUCTION
Lobo called for a meeting in the station master's office.
Present were:
Rajesh Naidu (compartment mate)
Captain Rajiv Sharma (traveling officer)
Vikram Sharma (platform attendant, witness)
Mohan Lal (Gupta's secretary)
Station Master R.N. Singh
Dr. Patnagar (railway medical officer)
It was 10:00 PM. Outside, the night was complete, the stars visible above Mercy Station in a way they wouldn't be visible in a city. The isolation of the station was almost physical—the sense that they were far from help, far from authorities who might intervene, far from anything that might protect them.
Lobo stood at the front of the office with his notebook and his assembled evidence. He spoke quietly, with the precision of someone who'd spent nine years learning how to reconstruct crimes from evidence.
"Harendra Kumar Gupta died this morning at 7:27 AM," Lobo began. "The official narrative is that he fell from the train as it pulled into Mercy Station. The official narrative is that it was an accident. But the evidence suggests something very different."
He held up Dr. Patnagar's preliminary autopsy findings. "The victim suffered a 1.5-inch penetrating wound to the left temporal region. This wound is consistent with impact from a blunt instrument with a defined contact point—a hammer, perhaps, or a similar object. The wound is not consistent with impact from a platform edge. The wound is not consistent with a fall. The wound is consistent with deliberate violence."
He paused to let that statement settle.
"Furthermore," Lobo continued, "the victim's legs were fractured post-mortem. This indicates the body was transported after death, handled roughly, possibly thrown into a vehicle. This is not what happens when someone falls. This is what happens when a body is being moved by people who are not concerned with dignity or evidence preservation."
He turned to Rajesh Naidu. "You were in the compartment with the victim. You were the last person to see him alive. The platform attendant, Vikram Sharma, observed what appeared to be a push from inside the compartment. The compartment geometry suggests that if someone were standing at the correct position and pushed with full force, the impact would have been devastating."
Naidu's face had gone very pale. His hands, which had been steady throughout the day, were now trembling visibly.
"I believe you pushed him," Lobo said quietly. "I believe you pushed him because he discovered something you couldn't allow him to report. I believe he discovered that you had been embezzling approximately ₹50,000 from covert foreign assistance accounts. I believe he was traveling to Delhi to report this to the Central Bureau."
"That's insane," Naidu said. "I don't know what you're talking about."
But he was trembling too much for the denial to carry weight.
Lobo turned to Captain Rajiv Sharma. "You knew Rajesh Naidu. You were traveling on the same train as the victim. You were positioned in the first-class lounge, a convenient location from which to monitor what was happening in the compartments. I believe you were there to ensure that the operation went smoothly. I believe you were there to coordinate what happened after the push."
Captain Rajiv's professional composure began to crack. His jaw tightened. His eyes became focused, intense.
"I believe this operation was coordinated by someone in the military apparatus," Lobo continued. "I believe you were following orders. I believe your superior officers authorized this operation because the victim's knowledge of the covert foreign assistance program represented a security threat. I believe you were told that ensuring his silence was a matter of national importance."
The captain stood. "I think I need a lawyer."
"Sit down," Lobo said quietly. But it wasn't a request. It was a statement of authority, and the captain, trained in military hierarchy, responded to it by sitting.
Lobo turned to the rest of the room. "The victim was wearing shoes this morning. After the push, the victim lost consciousness. His body fell to the platform. One of his shoes remained on his foot. The other shoe—his left shoe—fell in the compartment."
He held up the shoe he'd found in compartment three. "Someone entered that compartment after the push. Someone collected the left shoe. Someone carried that shoe to the platform, and someone—either while the body was still being positioned or immediately after—placed that shoe in the victim's right hand."
He paused. "That is not the action of someone trying to hide a crime. That is the action of someone trying to communicate something. That is the action of someone saying: 'I did this. I'm not hiding it. I understand the details. I understand the coordination.'"
"The five-rupee note in his other hand carries the same message. The body positioned near the junction box carries the same message. Everything about the crime scene is not an attempt to hide the crime. Everything about the crime scene is a demonstration that the crime was coordinated, intentional, and executed by someone with knowledge and authority."
Lobo closed his notebook. "I'm going to recommend that the magistrate issue arrest warrants for Rajesh Naidu for the murder of Harendra Kumar Gupta. I'm going to recommend that Captain Rajiv Sharma be held as an accessory to the crime and questioned regarding the coordination of the operation. And I'm going to recommend that this case be elevated to the national level because it involves military personnel and covert government operations."
The silence that followed was absolute.
Then Vikram Sharma began to cry. Mohan Lal looked relieved, as though a weight he'd been carrying all day had finally been placed down. Station Master Singh seemed to be calculating the implications for his own position.
And then the telephone rang.

CHAPTER TEN: THE MACHINERY RESPONDS
Station Master Singh answered the telephone. He listened for perhaps thirty seconds without saying anything. Then he handed the telephone to Lobo.
"It's for you," Singh said. "From Delhi."
Lobo took the telephone. The voice on the other end was professional, bureaucratic, the voice of someone accustomed to speaking with authority.
"Inspector Lobo, this is Deputy Director Vikram Chatterjee, Railway Board Administration. I'm calling regarding the Mercy Station investigation."
"Yes, sir," Lobo said.
"I understand you've made significant progress. That's excellent work. However, I'm calling to inform you that the investigation is now suspended pending administrative review from the highest levels."
"Sir, I believe I have sufficient evidence for arrests. I believe—"
"Inspector Lobo, I'm not going to repeat myself. The investigation is suspended. You are to stop all investigative activities immediately. You are to not make any statements to the magistrate, any press, or any officials outside of the railway system. And you are to return to Delhi immediately."
"Sir, this is a murder investigation. The evidence is clear. The victim's evidence is clear. I have witnesses. I have physical evidence. I have an autopsy that demonstrates deliberate violence."
"Inspector, the autopsy has been revised. The preliminary findings were determined to be inaccurate. The revised findings support the accidental death narrative. That is the official position."
"Sir, the revised findings contradict the evidence—"
"I'm not interested in your contradiction. I'm informing you of the official position. You will comply with this order, or you will find your career at the Central Bureau of Investigation very short indeed. Do you understand?"
Lobo understood. He understood perfectly.
"Yes, sir," he said. "I understand."
"Good. Leave Mercy Station by the 6:00 AM train. Report to my office when you arrive in Delhi. And Inspector? Congratulations on your thorough investigation. You did your job excellently. But this case is beyond both of our authority levels now."
The line went dead.
Lobo set down the telephone and looked at the faces of the people gathered in the office. They understood too. The machinery had spoken. The machinery had decided. And the machinery's decisions were not to be questioned.
By morning, Rajesh Naidu would be released. Captain Rajiv Sharma would disappear back to his military duties. Vikram Sharma would be told that the case was closed, that everything was fine, that he should forget about the push he thought he'd seen. The pressure would be applied subtly—a suggestion here, a threat there—until everyone who'd witnessed something was convinced they'd misremembered.
And Harendra Kumar Gupta would be dead with his death officially classified as accidental, with his investigation officially closed, with his truth officially buried.
Lobo understood that his career had just transformed. He was no longer an investigator. He was now a man who understood something he wasn't supposed to understand. He was now a liability to the system.
And liabilities to the system experienced difficulties.
He looked at Vikram Sharma, who was still crying. He looked at Mohan Lal, who was staring at the floor. He looked at Dr. Patnagar, who was looking at his shoes with the expression of a man who understood he'd made a choice that would define the rest of his life.
And he understood that this moment—this moment where the truth had been articulated and then immediately suppressed—was the moment that would break something inside him. Not his spirit. Not his morality. But his belief that systems could be reformed from within, that truth could be defended by individuals, that justice could exist in institutions designed to protect power.
