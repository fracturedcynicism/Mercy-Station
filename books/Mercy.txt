PART ONE: THE DEATH
CHAPTER ONE: SEVEN FIFTEEN
The Lucknow-to-Mercy express was exactly twelve minutes late, which meant it pulled into Mercy Station at 7:27 AM instead of 7:15. This detail would matter later, though no one knew it yet.
Vikram Sharma was on the platform when the train arrived. He was twenty-five years old and had been working as a platform attendant at Mercy Station for three years, long enough to understand the rhythms of the schedule but not long enough to be cynical about delays. He stood near the first-class coaches, ready to assist with luggage, ready to process tickets, ready for the morning routine that would define his day.
The train pulled in with the screech of brakes and the settling of a machine that had been moving for six hours and was now preparing to stop. Compartment doors opened. Passengers began to descend.
Most passengers descended normally—they stepped down from the compartment, their feet finding the steps with the muscle memory of people who'd done this many times. But the passenger from compartment three didn't descend normally.
Later, when Vikram tried to explain what he'd seen, he struggled with the language. He said the man "fell." He said the man "lost his balance." He said the man "simply went down." But what he'd actually seen, what his body understood even if his mind couldn't articulate it, was that the man had been pushed.
There was a moment—the smallest fraction of a second—where the man in the doorway seemed to be receiving force from behind. Not stumbling. Not tripping. Receiving force. His body moved forward and down with the momentum of impact rather than the momentum of losing balance.
And then his head struck the platform.
Vikram heard the sound. He would hear that sound for the rest of his life in dreams and in moments of sudden recall. It was the sound of bone and stone meeting at velocity. It was the sound of something breaking that shouldn't break.
The man lay very still.
Vikram ran toward the body. Other people on the platform had seen something was wrong—they were pointing, calling out. The train conductor appeared. A porter emerged. Within seconds, there was a small crowd gathered around the body, and Vikram was pushing through them, his training asserting itself—he was a railway employee, the man was on railway property, this was his responsibility.
The man was in his sixties, dressed in a simple dhoti and kurta, his grey hair matted with blood. His head was resting on the small junction box that controlled the electrical switching for the platform lights. His right hand held a five-rupee note. His left shoe was missing.
"Don't move him," said a voice from behind Vikram. It was the train conductor, Rajesh Kumar, an older man with the authority of someone who'd been conducting trains for twenty years. "Railway protocol. No one moves the body until investigation is complete."
"Is he alive?" asked one of the passengers.
"No," Vikram said, and he knew it was true by looking at the angle of the man's neck, the absence of breathing, the specific stillness that death imposed on a body. "He's dead."
The morning at Mercy Station, which had begun as routine, transformed in that instant into something that would ripple outward through the system. The platform attendant would call the station master. The station master would call railway police. Railway police would call the CBI. And by 8:00 AM, the machinery of investigation would be set in motion.
But at 7:27 AM, the morning after a man had been pushed from a moving train, all that existed was confusion, shock, and the specific confusion of people trying to understand what they'd just witnessed.
---
CHAPTER TWO: EIGHT O'CLOCK
John Lobo arrived at Mercy Station at 8:03 AM.
He was thirty-two years old, brilliant in the way of people who'd made investigating their entire life, and ambitious in the way that brilliance often generates. He'd been with the Central Bureau of Investigation for nine years, and he'd closed enough impossible cases that his superiors had begun to notice him. The telegram recalling him from Delhi had arrived at his office at 7:30 AM, with instructions to proceed to Mercy Station immediately to investigate an accidental death.
But the way the telegram was worded suggested it wasn't accidental.
"Investigate death of political figure, Mercy Station, presumed fall from train. Death occurs under unclear circumstances. Determine whether accident or foul play. Full authority granted. Report directly to headquarters."
The language was bureaucratic, but bureaucratic language had grammatical implications. The phrase "under unclear circumstances" meant someone had already noted the circumstances were unclear. The phrase "determine whether accident or foul play" meant the determination hadn't been made yet. And the phrase "full authority granted" meant Lobo was being given permission to proceed without the usual restrictions that governed investigations.
Which meant someone at a very high level was concerned that something might need investigating properly.
Lobo was driven from the station to Mercy Station in a police car. The driver was a local constable named Ashok, who'd been assigned to support the investigation. As they drove, Ashok explained what was known:
"The man's name was Harendra Kumar Gupta. Sixty-two years old. Scholar and political figure. He was traveling from Lucknow to Delhi on the express. He was in first-class compartment three. He fell from the compartment as the train pulled into the station. The platform attendant says it looked like he lost his balance. The compartment mate says the same thing."
"Who's the compartment mate?" Lobo asked.
"A businessman named Rajesh Naidu. From Calcutta. He was in the compartment with Gupta."
"And he saw the death?"
"He says he was gathering his luggage and heard a commotion. By the time he looked, Gupta was on the platform."
Lobo made a note. A compartment mate who didn't see the death but heard it. A witness who could corroborate the fall or contradict it, depending on what had actually happened. And Lobo's instinct—honed by nine years of investigation—was already suggesting that something wasn't right with the story.
The station master's office at Mercy was small, crowded with people. The body had been moved to a side room—temporarily stored until the autopsy could be performed by Dr. Patnagar, the railway medical officer who served three stations in the region. Standing in the main office were:
Vikram Sharma, the platform attendant, looking young and frightened
Rajesh Naidu, the compartment mate, looking composed but nervous
Station Master R.N. Singh, looking like a man who wished he were somewhere else
Mohan Lal, identified as Gupta's secretary and traveling companion
A military officer, Captain Rajiv Sharma, who'd apparently been traveling on the same train
Lobo took in the room with the speed of someone trained to observe. The body language. The positioning. The way people's eyes moved when they were uncomfortable. The way Naidu kept his hands very still, as though controlling them required concentration.
"I'm CBI Inspector John Lobo," he announced, and the room quieted. "I'm here to investigate the death of Harendra Kumar Gupta. I'm going to need to speak with each of you individually. Station Master Singh, I'll need a private room where I can conduct interviews."
Singh nodded. He was perhaps sixty, with the bearing of someone who'd spent decades managing small crises and was now facing something that seemed larger than his experience could accommodate. "You can use the compartment office. It's small but private."
"And I'll need access to the compartment where the death occurred."
"It's sealed," Singh said. "We locked it after the body was removed."
"Good. I'll need to examine it thoroughly."
Lobo gestured to Vikram Sharma. "You first. Come with me."
The compartment office was exactly what Lobo expected—a small room with a desk, two chairs, a window looking out onto the platform. Lobo sat behind the desk. Vikram sat across from him, his hands trembling slightly.
"I need to understand exactly what you saw," Lobo said quietly. "Not what you think happened. Not what someone told you happened. What you actually observed. Take your time. Be precise."
Vikram took a breath. "I was standing on the platform. Near the first-class compartments. The train was pulling in. People were preparing to descend. The compartment three door opened. I saw a man—the man who died, I understand now—standing in the doorway. He was preparing to step down."
"Could you see into the compartment?"
"Partially. I could see the interior past him."
"Was anyone else in the compartment?"
"There appeared to be another person. A younger man. But the body of the first man blocked most of my view."
"Go on."
"The first man began to descend. But it didn't look like a normal descent. It looked like... like he was being pushed. His body moved forward very quickly. Too quickly for a normal step. And he fell. His head struck the platform junction box."
Lobo watched Vikram's face carefully. The young man was telling the truth. Not the polished truth of someone who'd been coached, but the raw truth of someone who'd seen something disturbing and was still processing it.
"You said it looked like he was being pushed. What exactly do you mean?"
"I don't know how else to describe it. It was the speed. The angle. The way his body moved. Someone was pushing him."
"Did you see the person pushing him?"
"No. The body of the man blocked the view. I only saw the result—the man falling forward and down."
Lobo nodded. "Thank you. I may need to speak with you again. For now, please stay available. Don't leave Mercy Station."
Vikram left. Lobo sat alone in the compartment office and made detailed notes:
Platform attendant's account: Observed fall from compartment three. Describes motion as "pushed" rather than "stumbled." Did not see person delivering the push. Reaction genuine, not rehearsed. Reliable witness so far.
Next was Rajesh Naidu.
The businessman was perhaps forty, with the bearing of someone accustomed to success and the careful movements of someone trying to appear unconcerned. He sat across from Lobo and folded his hands on the desk.
"I understand you were in compartment three with Harendra Kumar Gupta," Lobo began.
"I was. We shared the compartment from Lucknow."
"Can you describe your activities this morning, starting from when you woke?"
Naidu's recitation was smooth, practiced-sounding: "I woke around 6:30 AM, as one does on trains. The motion wakes you. I used the compartment toilet. I washed. I began gathering my luggage and personal items, as one does when approaching a station. Mr. Gupta was lying on his berth, reading a book. I heard some commotion outside. I looked out the window and saw people gathering on the platform. I didn't understand what was happening. I finished gathering my things and prepared to descend."
"You didn't attempt to descend before the commotion?"
"No. I was still organizing my belongings."
"Did Mr. Gupta seem unwell this morning? Dizzy? Unstable?"
"Not at all. He seemed quite normal. We'd spoken briefly while I was gathering my things. He mentioned he was ready to descend."
"Did he seem nervous? Anxious?"
Naidu paused fractionally. "Not particularly. He seemed like a man preparing to exit a train. No more, no less."
Lobo watched that pause. It was microseconds long, but it existed. The pause before "not particularly." The hesitation before the reassurance. Naidu had something to conceal about Gupta's emotional state.
"And you didn't see him fall?"
"I heard the commotion. By the time I looked, people were already gathering around the body."
"Were you in the doorway at any point?"
"The compartment doorway? No. I was collecting my bags from the upper berth."
"So you were facing away from the doorway when the death occurred?"
"I suppose so, yes."
Lobo made notes:
Compartment mate account: Smooth, rehearsed. Claims Gupta seemed normal but hesitates when asked about emotional state. Story places him away from doorway during death. Cannot verify. Convenient positioning—back turned to death, unable to see assailant. Likely involved or positioned specifically to not see assailant.
"Thank you, Mr. Naidu. I may need to speak with you again."
After Naidu left, Lobo requested to see the compartment before he interviewed anyone else. Station Master Singh led him to the first-class coach, which was still parked at the platform, sealed from public access.
Compartment three was exactly as it had been left after the body was removed. Two upper berths, two lower berths. A window facing the direction of travel. A door facing the corridor. Everything was standard, everything was correct, and yet something about the compartment felt wrong.
Lobo pulled out his measuring tape.
He began with the dimensions. 2.1 meters long, 1.8 meters wide, 2.2 meters high. Standard measurements for first-class meter-gauge compartments. He documented these carefully in his notebook.
But as he was measuring, he noticed something that made him pause. The window, which should be at a consistent height along the length of the compartment, seemed to be at different heights when he measured from different positions. He measured again. And again.
The window was 4 centimeters higher on the compartment's forward end than on its rear end.
That shouldn't be possible in a standard compartment. Lobo looked at the walls. They seemed straight. He looked at the floor. It seemed level. But the window measurements were undeniably inconsistent.
He examined the floor more carefully. There were faint scratches near the door, as though something heavy had been dragged across the compartment floor.
And on the floor, in the corner near the window, he found something else: a single shoe, leather, oxford style, expensive. One shoe. The other was presumably in the dead man's hand—the shoe that had been reported as being held in his right hand, though no one had explained why a man with a broken skull would be clutching his own shoe.
Lobo photographed the shoe. He photographed the scratch marks. He photographed the window from multiple angles, documenting the height discrepancy.
When he was finished, he sat on the lower berth and tried to understand what the compartment was telling him.
A man couldn't fall from a compartment and end up with one shoe in his hand and the other shoe on his foot. That was physically impossible. The shoe would have to be deliberately placed. Which meant the body had been arranged after death.
If the body had been arranged, then the death hadn't been simple accident. It had been coordinated. It had been planned.
The question was: who had planned it, and why?
---
CHAPTER THREE: MORNING INTERVIEWS
By 10:00 AM, Lobo had established a timeline and begun to see the contradictions.
Vikram Sharma said he saw a push. Rajesh Naidu said there was no push—only a fall. One of them was lying, or one of them hadn't seen what they claimed to have seen.
But before Lobo could pursue that contradiction, he needed more information. He needed to understand the victim. He needed to understand the motive. He needed to understand why a sixty-two-year-old scholar and political figure was important enough that someone wanted him dead in a coordinated operation on a moving train.
Mohan Lal was the secretary, which meant Mohan likely knew more about Gupta's life than anyone else present. Lobo requested his interview next.
Mohan was perhaps forty, thin, with the bearing of someone accustomed to serving others. He sat across from Lobo and waited to be questioned.
"Tell me about Harendra Kumar Gupta," Lobo began.
Mohan took a breath. His voice, when he spoke, was controlled but carried an undertone of grief. "He was a scholar. A brilliant man. He taught Sanskrit at the university in Lucknow. He was also politically active—he believed in Integral Humanism, the philosophy of uplifting the poorest members of society."
"Was he a controversial figure?"
"Some people viewed his philosophy as radical. Others supported it entirely. It depends on one's perspective regarding wealth distribution and social hierarchy."
"Why was he traveling to Delhi?"
Mohan hesitated. Just slightly, but the hesitation was there.
"He had meetings scheduled," Mohan said finally.
"What kind of meetings?"
"With other party leaders. Political discussions."
Lobo waited. Silence was often more effective than questions. People felt the need to fill silence, and when they did, they sometimes said things they hadn't intended to say.
Mohan filled the silence. "He was concerned about certain financial irregularities. He wanted to discuss them with trusted colleagues."
"What kind of financial irregularities?"
"I'm not certain of the details. Mr. Gupta was careful about what he discussed and with whom. But I know he'd been investigating something related to party finances."
"Embezzlement?"
Mohan's expression flickered. Fear, quickly suppressed. "I couldn't say. I'm his secretary, not his confidant in all matters."
Lobo made a note. Gupta had been investigating financial irregularities. He'd been cautious about discussing them. He'd been on his way to Delhi to report them to someone he trusted. And Rajesh Naidu—Gupta's compartment mate—was a businessman who had access to Gupta throughout the night.
A scenario began to form in Lobo's mind. Not yet complete, not yet verified, but taking shape.
"Mr. Lal, where were you when Mr. Gupta fell?"
"I was in compartment four. Adjacent to his."
"Can anyone verify this?"
"The porter may have seen me. I asked him for water around 7:20, just before the train began to slow."
"Thank you."
After Mohan left, Lobo interviewed Station Master Singh. The station master was nervous, which was appropriate given that a death had occurred at his station under mysterious circumstances. But the nervousness had a particular quality—it wasn't just concern about his job (though Lobo was sure that was part of it). It was something else. A kind of controlled anxiety, as though Singh was monitoring his own reactions to ensure he didn't reveal something.
"Walk me through the morning," Lobo instructed.
"I was in my office. The train arrived. I heard commotion on the platform. I came out to investigate. There was a body. I secured the area and called the local police, who then called you."
"Did you see the death occur?"
"No. I was inside."
"Did you see Mr. Gupta at any point this morning?"
Singh hesitated. "When the train arrived, the passengers began to descend. I observed them from a distance. I saw a man in first-class clothing descend from compartment three."
"And then?"
"And then I heard the commotion."
"But you didn't see the fall itself?"
"No."
Lobo made a note of the contradiction. Singh had seen Gupta descend, but then claimed to not have seen the fall. But descending and falling were part of the same motion. If Singh saw Gupta descend, he should have seen Gupta fall, unless something happened in the interval—unless someone pushed Gupta and Singh looked away at exactly that moment.
Or unless Singh was lying.
"Mr. Singh, has anyone contacted you since the death? Anyone official? Anyone who suggested that your cooperation might be important?"
Singh's face went very still. "What do you mean?"
"I mean: has anyone called you on the telephone? Has anyone sent a message? Has anyone visited you?"
"No."
The lie was obvious. Singh had received a contact. Lobo could see it in the man's eyes, in the tension of his jaw, in the careful control of his voice.
"If someone does contact you," Lobo said quietly, "I need you to tell me immediately. This is a murder investigation. Obstruction of justice is a serious crime."
"Of course," Singh said. But his hands were trembling.
Finally, Lobo interviewed Captain Rajiv Sharma.
The military officer was perhaps thirty, with the bearing of someone trained in precision and the expression of someone carrying secrets. He sat across from Lobo with the posture of someone prepared for interrogation.
"I understand you were traveling on the express this morning," Lobo began.
"I was. I'm stationed in Lucknow. I was returning to my base near Mercy."
"Which compartment were you in?"
"I was in the first-class lounge car, most of the night. I moved there after dinner because I was reading and didn't want to disturb my compartment mate."
"Can anyone verify this?"
"The steward may have seen me. I ordered tea from him around midnight and again at 6:00 AM."
"And you were in the lounge when the train pulled into Mercy Station?"
"I was. I only moved to descend when the train had completely stopped."
Lobo studied the officer. His account was smooth, professional, the kind of account given by someone trained in giving accounts. But there was a detail that bothered Lobo. Captain Rajiv had given very specific times—the tea at midnight and at 6:00 AM. These were too specific, too convenient. He was establishing an alibi, but he was doing it in a way that suggested he'd been coached.
"Captain Sharma, do you know Rajesh Naidu?"
For just a moment—the smallest fraction of a second—Captain Rajiv's composure slipped. His eyes widened infinitesimally. Then the professional mask returned.
"I don't believe so."
"You've never met him?"
"Not that I'm aware of."
But the moment had happened. The recognition. The quickly suppressed response. Captain Rajiv Sharma knew Rajesh Naidu. And he'd been coached not to acknowledge it.
By 11:30 AM, Lobo had interviewed everyone present at Mercy Station who'd been on the train. What he had was a collection of accounts that didn't quite align. Vikram Sharma said he saw a push. Rajesh Naidu said there was no push—only a fall. Station Master Singh had seen Gupta descend but claimed not to have seen him fall. Captain Rajiv Sharma claimed to be in the lounge car but had shown recognition when asked about Rajesh Naidu. And Mohan Lal had admitted that Gupta was investigating financial irregularities.
Lobo went to the side room where the body was being stored and waited for Dr. Patnagar, the railway medical officer, to arrive for the autopsy.
---
CHAPTER FOUR: THE AUTOPSY
Dr. Patnagar was perhaps fifty-five, with the bearing of someone who'd spent decades examining dead bodies and had learned not to feel much about it. He arrived at Mercy Station at 12:15 PM with his medical bag and an assistant, and he set about examining the body with the professional efficiency of someone who'd done this many times.
Lobo watched from the corner of the room, documenting everything in his notebook.
The autopsy was conducted on a metal table in a room adjacent to the station master's office. Dr. Patnagar began with external examination.
"Body of a male, approximately 62 years of age," Patnagar narrated, his voice taking on the specific clinical tone of professional documentation. "Dressed in dhoti and kurta. Head wound, left temporal region. Significant trauma. Single shoe on right foot. Blood on sole of right shoe. Left shoe missing."
"The shoe that's missing," Lobo interjected, "was it in the deceased's hand?"
"Yes. I documented that. Right hand holding left shoe. Unusual. Typically in a fall, the victim doesn't grasp their own shoe."
Patnagar continued. He examined the wound more closely, measuring it carefully. "Lacerated wound, left temporal region. Measuring 1.5 inches in depth. This is significant. A 1.5-inch penetrating wound is not consistent with impact trauma from a platform edge or pole. This is consistent with impact from a blunt instrument with a defined contact point. A hammer, perhaps. Or the corner of a heavy object."
Lobo made a note. A 1.5-inch penetrating wound. Not a fall. Not an accidental impact. Intentional impact from a defined weapon.
"Are you certain?" Lobo asked.
Patnagar looked up. "The shape of the wound. The depth. The characteristics of the tissue damage. Yes, I'm certain. This man was struck with a blunt object, not struck by an edge. The impact was significant and deliberate."
"Continue."
Patnagar proceeded to internal examination. He opened the chest, examined the organs, documented what he found.
"Lungs show signs of recent exertion. The heart shows no abnormalities. The liver shows signs of consumption of alcohol but not cirrhosis. Overall, the deceased was in reasonable health, which makes the trauma death more pronounced."
"What killed him?" Lobo asked.
"The head wound. There's significant intracranial bleeding. Death would have been very rapid, likely within minutes of impact. Possibly immediate."
"Could he have fallen and struck a platform edge hard enough to cause this wound?"
Patnagar looked at Lobo directly. "In theory, yes. But the wound characteristics don't support that narrative. A fall would produce jagged edges, multiple impact points, inconsistent depth. This wound has clean edges, a consistent depth, and characteristics consistent with a single, deliberate strike from a blunt instrument."
"Could someone striking him with such force produce an injury consistent with a fall?"
"Yes," Patnagar said. "If the object used was intended to appear as though it was impact trauma."
Patnagar continued the examination and discovered what would later matter: bilateral leg fractures with no hemorrhage at the fracture sites.
"These fractures occurred after death," Patnagar stated. "There's no bleeding because the heart wasn't pumping at the time the legs broke. This suggests the body was handled roughly after death. The legs may have been caught between something—a moving vehicle, perhaps, or machinery."
"He was transported after death?" Lobo asked.
"Yes. The legs were broken during transport. The fractures are post-mortem."
By 2:00 PM, Dr. Patnagar had completed his preliminary examination and had documented his findings in meticulous detail:
"Cause of death: severe head trauma from blunt force impact. Weapon: likely hammer or similar blunt object with defined contact point. The impact characteristics are not consistent with accidental fall from train. Post-mortem fractures to bilateral legs indicate body was transported after death. Blood on right shoe sole indicates body made contact with floor while being transported. Preliminary conclusion: death was not accidental. Death was the result of deliberate violence."
Lobo read the preliminary autopsy report and felt a clarity settle over him. This wasn't an accident. This was murder. And the murder had been coordinated—the body had been transported, handled, and positioned deliberately. The shoe in the hand was a deliberate arrangement. The body positioning near the junction box was deliberate. Everything about the crime scene suggested planning and coordination.
Now the question was: who had planned it, and why?
---
CHAPTER FIVE: THE COMPARTMENT MATHEMATICS
After the autopsy, Lobo returned to compartment three with a new focus.
If the body had been handled after death, if the legs had been broken during transport, then the compartment was the crime scene. The push must have occurred in the compartment or in the doorway. The struggle must have occurred in the compartment. Evidence of the crime might still exist in the compartment.
Lobo examined the floor systematically. He found the scratches he'd noticed before—marks as though something heavy had been dragged from the door toward the window. He photographed these marks from multiple angles. He measured the distance from the door to the window. 2.1 meters.
He examined the seats. The lower berths were undisturbed, but the upper berths bore signs of occupation—rumpled blankets, a book on one berth, personal items on another.
He examined the walls. The plaster was smooth, painted cream color. Nothing appeared to have been damaged.
But something nagged at Lobo. He returned to his measuring tape and began measuring the compartment's internal dimensions again. This time, he measured the distance from the floor to the ceiling using multiple reference points—at the door, at the window, at the rear of the compartment.
His measurements gave him different results depending on where he measured.
At the door: 2.2 meters.
At the window: 2.16 meters.
At the rear: 2.2 meters.
The window area was 4 centimeters lower than the door and rear areas. That shouldn't be possible in a standard compartment. The ceiling should be consistently high throughout the compartment.
He measured the window placement. The window frame seemed to be in the correct location relative to the compartment, but the interior dimensions suggested the compartment was subtly warped. The geometry was off.
He stood in the compartment and tried to imagine the crime. Naidu and Gupta sharing the compartment. Gupta standing to descend. Naidu pushing from behind.
If Naidu planted his feet at the exact right position—where the compartment geometry created maximum leverage—and pushed with his full body weight, the impact would have been devastating. The angled descent, combined with the compartment's distorted geometry, would have created the perfect angle for Gupta's head to strike something on the platform.
But Lobo still didn't understand why the shoe was in the hand. Why would Gupta—either before the push or after the fall—clutch his own shoe?
The answer came to him as he examined the floor more carefully.
There were two shoes in the compartment—not metaphorically, but literally. One shoe sat on the floor near the window. This must be Gupta's left shoe, fallen during the fall. The other shoe—Gupta's right shoe—was missing from the compartment but present on Gupta's foot on the platform.
Someone had entered the compartment after the push. Someone had collected evidence. Someone had removed Gupta's left shoe from the floor and carried it to the platform, where it had been deliberately placed in the corpse's hand as a message.
A message that said: This was planned. This was coordinated. Someone understood all the details.
Lobo documented everything photographically and in his notebook. By 4:00 PM, he had assembled a picture:
Gupta and Naidu shared compartment three.
Gupta stood to descend at Mercy Station.
Naidu pushed Gupta from behind with significant force.
Gupta's head impacted something hard—likely the platform edge, but possibly something else, something carried specifically for the purpose.
Gupta died immediately or very quickly.
The body was handled post-mortem—transported, its legs broken in the process.
Gupta's left shoe was collected from the compartment and placed in the corpse's hand.
The body was positioned near the junction box, arranged to look accidental.
A five-rupee note was placed in the corpse's other hand.
This level of coordination suggested more than one person was involved. Naidu couldn't have pushed Gupta and also transported the body and also positioned it on the platform. Someone else had to be involved. Someone on the outside. Someone coordinating the operation.
The question was: who?
---
CHAPTER SIX: THE PRESSURE
At 4:30 PM, Station Master Singh came to Lobo with a message.
"There's been a telephone call," Singh said, and he was clearly nervous. "From Delhi. From the Railway Board's administrative office. They want to know what the preliminary findings are regarding Mr. Gupta's death."
"What did you tell them?" Lobo asked.
"Nothing. I said I would have the investigating officer contact them directly."
"Did they say anything else?"
Singh hesitated. "They said the investigation should be concluded quickly. They said it was important for the railway's reputation that we establish the death as accidental. They said... they said that continuing a lengthy investigation could cause problems."
Lobo understood. The machinery was beginning to move. Someone in Delhi wanted the investigation shut down. Someone wanted the death classified as accidental. Someone wanted the truth buried.
"Did they give their name?" Lobo asked.
"No. It was an official voice. Very professional. Very polite. But the message was clear."
"Thank you for telling me," Lobo said. "If they call again, put them through to me directly."
But as Singh left, Lobo understood that the investigation had just become more dangerous.
At 5:00 PM, Dr. Patnagar came to Lobo with revised autopsy findings.
The revised findings were different from the preliminary examination. The 1.5-inch penetrating wound was now described as "consistent with impact from a platform edge or similar hard surface." The post-mortem fractures were now described as "possibly consistent with the body impact from the fall." The blood on the shoe sole was now described as "potentially from contact with the platform during the fall."
Everything that had pointed to deliberate violence now pointed to accidental death.
"Dr. Patnagar," Lobo said carefully, "these findings contradict your preliminary examination. What changed?"
Patnagar wouldn't meet Lobo's eyes. "Upon reflection, the injuries are consistent with the fall narrative. The initial assessment was based on insufficient data."
"Did someone contact you?" Lobo asked. "Did someone pressure you to change your findings?"
"No," Patnagar said. But the lie was obvious.
"Dr. Patnagar, if someone is pressuring you to alter your findings, that's obstruction of justice. It's a serious crime. And it's a crime I can investigate."
Patnagar finally met Lobo's eyes. What Lobo saw there was fear. Pure, uncomplicated fear.
"My daughter," Patnagar said quietly. "She's engaged to be married. The family of her fiancé is... well-placed. Influential. I received a telephone call. A very polite telephone call. Someone simply mentioned that if my findings created complications, it might affect my daughter's engagement. That her fiancé's family might decide to end the engagement."
"And you changed your findings because of this threat?"
"What was I supposed to do? My daughter's happiness depends on this marriage. The family is prominent. They're offering her a good life. I'm a small-town medical officer. What could I do against people like that?"
Lobo understood. The machinery didn't need to be obviously threatening. It just needed to identify what someone cared about—family, career, security—and then suggest that things might not go well for those people if cooperation wasn't forthcoming.
"I need your preliminary findings," Lobo said. "The real ones. Not the revised ones."
"I can't. I've already submitted the revised findings to the station master. They're official now."
"Then I'm going to need you to testify in front of a magistrate that the revised findings were pressured and inaccurate. Can you do that?"
Patnagar was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "Yes. If you can protect me."
"I'll do my best," Lobo said, though he understood that protecting Patnagar might be impossible. The machinery was larger than both of them.
---
CHAPTER SEVEN: THE LETTER
At 6:00 PM, Mohan Lal came to Lobo privately.
"There's something I need to tell you," Mohan said. "Something I was afraid to mention this morning."
They sat in the compartment office with the door closed. Mohan pulled from his bag a letter, dated February 10, 1968—more than a month before the death.
Lobo read the letter, written in Gupta's handwriting:
"I have discovered serious irregularities in the financial accounts of our organization. Money has been moving through our accounts that we did not authorize. Money that came with conditions—conditions that compromised our political independence. I have traced this money back to foreign sources, specifically to military-coordinated operations that were using the Janata Party as a cover for unauthorized operations.
"More specifically, I have discovered that one of our own—a man named Rajesh Naidu—has been embezzling funds from these accounts for personal use. He has taken approximately ₹50,000 in what appears to be personal profit from the foreign assistance program. If I report this, it will expose not just his embezzlement but the entire covert funding mechanism."
The letter went on to detail Gupta's intentions to report both the embezzlement and the broader financial irregularities to the Central Bureau in Delhi. It concluded:
"I am aware that making this report may be dangerous. But remaining silent is complicity. I will not remain silent."
The letter was unsigned.
"He gave you this?" Lobo asked.
"He gave it to me for safekeeping. He said if anything happened to him, the letter should be given to the authorities. But I was afraid. I didn't know what to do. This morning, when I heard he was dead, I thought... I thought maybe I should give it to you."
"You did the right thing," Lobo said. "How long have you had this letter?"
"Since February."
"And you never told anyone?"
"Who could I tell? If I told the authorities, I would be implicating Rajesh Naidu. If I told the railway officials, I would be exposing corruption inside the system. I didn't know what to do."
Lobo understood. Mohan had been trapped between complicity and danger, and he'd chosen the path of silence. But Lobo's presence—the CBI investigator asking questions—had changed the calculation. Now silence was more dangerous than speech.
"This changes everything," Lobo said. "Now I understand the motive. Naidu was embezzling. Gupta discovered it. Gupta was going to report it. Naidu couldn't allow that."
"But Rajesh didn't act alone," Mohan said. "He couldn't have. The coordination suggests someone official was involved. Someone who could arrange for evidence to be removed. Someone who could pressure medical officers to revise their findings."
"Someone in the military," Lobo said, thinking of Captain Rajiv Sharma's recognition when asked about Naidu. "Someone with access to personnel and resources."
"Yes," Mohan agreed. "Someone like that."

CHAPTER EIGHT: THE CONNECTIONS
By 8:00 PM, Lobo had assembled enough evidence to begin understanding the full scope of the operation.
Rajesh Naidu had been embezzling from covert foreign assistance accounts. Harendra Kumar Gupta had discovered the embezzlement. Gupta was traveling to Delhi to report both the embezzlement and the broader financial irregularities to the Central Bureau.
Captain Rajiv Sharma had known Rajesh Naidu. This connection suggested military coordination of the foreign assistance program. The fact that Captain Rajiv had been on the same train as Gupta suggested he was there specifically to monitor the situation or to ensure something happened.
The operation required coordination. The push was executed by Naidu in the compartment. But someone on the outside coordinated the removal of evidence, the handling of the body, the positioning on the platform. Someone arranged for Captain Rajiv to be present. Someone contacted Dr. Patnagar and pressured him to revise his findings. Someone contacted Station Master Singh and suggested the investigation should be concluded quickly.
That someone was higher up in the system. That someone had authority that extended beyond the railway.
Lobo requested a private meeting with Vikram Sharma.
The young platform attendant was nervous when he entered the compartment office. He'd clearly been thinking about the morning's events, replaying them in his mind, wondering if his statement had been correct or if he'd misremembered.
"I need to ask you something," Lobo said. "Has anyone spoken to you since this morning? Anyone official? Anyone who suggested you might be mistaken about what you saw?"
Vikram's face confirmed it before he spoke. "Yes. A man came to my house. He was official-looking, but I didn't catch his name. He said that eyewitness accounts at scenes like this are often unreliable. He said people often think they see things they didn't actually see. He suggested that what I thought was a push might have been the natural motion of someone stepping down. He suggested that I might be misremembering."
"And has your memory changed?"
"No," Vikram said. "I still see what I saw. I still think it was a push. But I'm frightened. This man implied that insisting on my account could cause problems for my family. My brother works in railway administration. The man suggested my brother's employment could be affected if I continued to insist on a narrative that contradicted the official account."
"Has anyone contacted your brother?"
"I don't know. I haven't asked him. I've been too afraid."
Lobo understood. The system was efficient. It didn't need to threaten everyone openly. It just needed to suggest to each person that their cooperation mattered, that their silence mattered, that their revision of their own perceptions mattered.
But Vikram had resisted that pressure, and for that, Lobo respected him.
"You did the right thing telling me this," Lobo said. "And I'm going to need you to testify to these threats. I'm going to call a magistrate and have you give a formal statement about both the death and the pressure you've received to change your account."
"Will I be protected?" Vikram asked.
"I'm going to do everything in my power to protect you," Lobo said. But he understood that his power might be limited.
By 9:00 PM, Lobo had documented:
The 1.5-inch penetrating wound (evidence of deliberate violence)
The post-mortem fractures and body transport
The compartment geometry and the evidence of deliberate push
The embezzlement motive (Naidu had ₹50,000 to lose)
The covert foreign assistance program (broader motive for the operation)
The military coordination (Captain Rajiv Sharma's involvement)
The pressure on witnesses (Dr. Patnagar, Station Master Singh, Vikram Sharma)
The pressure on the investigation itself (calls from Delhi suggesting quick closure)
All of it pointed to a coordinated murder with institutional involvement.
Now he needed to construct the full narrative. He needed to understand not just how Gupta died, but why the system wanted to cover up his death. And he needed to do it before the machinery moved against him.
